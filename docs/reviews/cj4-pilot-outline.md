# Pilot track outline: "Talk to anyone" (cj4, Job 5)

> **DRAFT FOR EDITORS.** Nothing here is pack-ready. The content team rewrites it into the topic pack format
> (`hook`, `teach`, `example.parts` + `spot`, `guided[]`, `takeaway`, `quest`, spaced `items`) and runs the

Date: 23 Oct 2026. Branch: `ideas/cj4-pilot-outline`. Scope: 8 ideas on conversation skills for Indian 18-25s.

## Summary

- 8 ideas have a peer-reviewed source we opened and quoted. 3 are strong for a 2-minute lesson (follow-up
  questions, deeper talk, disagreeing kindly). 3 are moderate (names, ending a chat, asking seniors for advice).
  Listening is mixed (small effects). Group chats are weak and rest on an inference.
- Nearly every study used US, UK or Israeli samples, mostly with strangers, in labs or online. Nothing we found
  tests Indian settings such as elders at family functions or hierarchy at work. Every lesson below adds a scene
  from Indian life, but the evidence does not cover those scenes directly. Editors need to say so in the copy.
- One popular "fact" did not hold up and is left out: "a phone on the table ruins the conversation" failed a close
  replication (Linares & Sellier 2021).

## 1. Ask one more question

### Evidence

| Claim | Source quote | Source |
|---|---|---|
| People who ask more questions, mainly follow-up questions, are liked more. | "people who ask more questions, particularly follow-up questions, are better liked by their conversation partners." | Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017), *JPSP* 113(3), 430-452. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000097 (read from the full-text PDF at https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/novelty/2017-huang.pdf) |
| A follow-up question asks the other person to say more about what they just said. | "We define follow-up questions as questions that encourage the partner to elaborate on the content of their prior conversational turn" | Same paper |
| People do not expect this effect. | "people do not anticipate that question-asking increases interpersonal liking." | Same paper |
| Speed daters who asked more follow-ups got more second-date agreement. | "speed daters who ask more follow-up questions during their dates are more likely to elicit agreement for second dates from their partners" | Same paper |
| Limit (authors' idea, not tested): too many questions with no sharing back may backfire. | "When one asks too many questions without reciprocating self-disclosure, liking may decrease." | Same paper, discussion |

**Strength: strong.** 3 studies of live chats plus speed-dating data. US samples. The effect works through
seeming responsive ("listening, understanding, validation, and care"), so the lesson must frame questions as
genuine interest, not a trick.

### Hook question

In a first chat, who is usually liked more?
- a) The one with the best stories.
- b) **The one who asks more follow-up questions.** (correct)
- c) The one who talks about themselves the most.

### Teach card (26 words)

> A follow-up question picks up what the other person just said and asks for more. In studies, people who asked
> more of them were liked more.

### Show me

Ask: "Tap the follow-up question."

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| 1 | Meera: "I went home to Nashik this weekend." |
| 2 | Arjun: "Nice. What did you do there?" |
| 3 | Meera: "Helped my dad at his shop. Sale week." |
| 4 | Arjun: "What was the busiest part?" **(spot)** |
| 5 | Arjun: "My mama has a shop too. Diwali week is chaos." |

Feedback: "Part 4 picks up 'shop' and 'sale week', so it is a follow-up. Part 5 shares something back, which
keeps it from turning into an interview."

### Guided try

A classmate says: "Just got back from my cousin's wedding in Jaipur." Which reply is a follow-up?

| Option | Correct | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| a) "How was it? Did anything funny happen?" | Yes | Right. It asks for more about the wedding they just mentioned. |
| b) "Cool. Did you finish the DBMS assignment?" | No | This changes the topic. A follow-up stays on what they said. |
| c) "Weddings are the best. Wait till you hear about my sister's sangeet..." | No, **misconception: "A better story of my own shows interest."** | A matching story moves the spotlight to you. Ask first, share after. |

### Do it today

In one conversation today, ask two follow-up questions before you talk about yourself. Then share one thing back.

Reflection prompt (next day): "What did you hear that you would have missed otherwise?"

### Later-day question

"Which of these is a follow-up question?" (Give a short exchange with one follow-up, one topic switch, one
yes/no question about something new.)

## 2. Show you are listening

### Evidence

| Claim | Source quote | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Active listening has three parts: paraphrase, questions, and staying engaged. | "Active listening involves restating a paraphrased version of the speaker's message, asking questions when appropriate, and maintaining moderate to high nonverbal conversational involvement." | Weger, Castle Bell, Minei & Robinson (2014), *International Journal of Listening* 28(1), 13-31. https://doi.org/10.1080/10904018.2013.813234 (read from a hosted copy of the article PDF: https://fellow.app/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/The-Relative-Effectiveness-of-Active-Listening-in-Initial-Interactions-1.pdf) |
| Active listening made people feel more understood than advice or "hmm". | "participants who received active listening responses felt more understood than participants who received either advice or simple acknowledgements." | Same paper |
| But it did not beat advice on satisfaction or liking; effects were small. | "although the effect sizes for these differences were small. Conversational satisfaction and social attractiveness did not differ between participants receiving active listening responses and participants receiving advice, however." | Same paper |
| A paraphrase alone did not make people feel understood in an earlier study. | "message paraphrases were associated with the social attractiveness of the listener but were not associated with participants' conversational satisfaction or perceptions of feeling understood by the listener." | Weger, Castle & Emmett (2010), *International Journal of Listening* 24. https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/1477 |
| Attentive, empathic, non-judging listening lowers the speaker's social anxiety. | "We predicted that high-quality (i.e., empathic, attentive, and nonjudgmental) listening reduces speakers' social anxiety" ... "We obtained consistent support for our hypotheses across five experiments" | Itzchakov, DeMarree, Kluger & Turjeman-Levi (2018), *PSPB* 44(5). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29347879/ |

**Strength: mixed.** "Feel understood" is supported in one study and not in another. Effects are small. Teach
the full package (say it back + ask + stay with them), not "paraphrase and you will be liked".

### Hook question

A friend is upset about a bad viva. Which reply tends to make people feel most understood?
- a) Quick advice on how to prepare next time.
- b) **Say back what you heard, then ask about it.** (correct)
- c) "Happens to everyone."

### Teach card (26 words)

> To show you are listening, say back the gist in your own words, ask about it, and stay with them. Advice can
> wait until they ask.

### Show me

Ask: "Tap the line that says it back."

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| 1 | Sana: "My manager redid my whole deck and didn't tell me." |
| 2 | Rohan closes his laptop and turns to her. |
| 3 | Rohan: "So he changed your work without a word to you." **(spot)** |
| 4 | Rohan: "What bugged you more, the changes or not being told?" |
| 5 | Rohan: "Want ideas, or did you just need to vent?" |

Feedback: "Part 3 says the gist back in Rohan's words. Part 4 asks about it. Part 5 checks before giving advice."

### Guided try

At a family function, Tanvi tells her cousin: "I didn't get placed in the first round." Which reply fits active
listening best?

| Option | Correct | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| a) "Apply off-campus. That's what worked for me." | No, **misconception: "Advice is the best way to show I care."** | Advice is not wrong, but in the study it did not make people feel as understood. |
| b) "So round one didn't go your way. How are you feeling about round two?" | Yes | It says the gist back and asks about it. |
| c) "Hmm." | No | A bare "hmm" made people feel the least understood. |

### Do it today

In one chat today, say back what someone told you in one line before you give any opinion.

Reflection prompt: "Did they correct you, or add more?"

### Later-day question

"Which three things make up active listening in the research?" (Order or pick-three item.)

### Myth to leave out

"A phone on the table ruins the conversation." A close replication failed: "In both studies, we failed to
replicate the original results on relationship quality." Linares & Sellier (2021), *PLoS One*.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34106931/. Copy can still say "turn to them", which is part of the
attention described in the Weger definition, without a phone claim.

## 3. Go one level deeper (small talk to real talk)

### Evidence

| Claim | Source quote | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Chats with strangers felt less awkward and more connecting than people expected. | "conversations between strangers felt less awkward, and created more connectedness and happiness, than the participants themselves expected" | Kardas, Kumar & Epley (2022), *JPSP* 122(3), 367-398. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34591541/ |
| People most overrate the awkwardness of deep talk. | "Participants were especially prone to overestimate how awkward deep conversations would be compared with shallow conversations" | Same |
| The reason: they expect others to care less than they do. | "participants expected others to care less about their disclosures in conversation than others actually did" | Same |
| After having both, people felt closer to deep-talk partners. | "they also felt more connected to deep conversation partners than shallow conversation partners after having both types of conversations" | Same |
| Knowing this changes choices. | "more calibrated expectations encourage deeper conversation" | Same |
| With close friends and family, predictions were more accurate. | "participants more accurately predicted the outcomes of their conversations when speaking with close friends, family, or partners whose care and interest is more clearly known" | Same |

**Strength: strong.** Many experiments, one consistent pattern. Limits: US strangers in set-up chats, with
questions the researchers chose. "Deep" in the study means personal and meaningful, not private facts.

### Hook question

Before a deep chat with someone new, most people guess it will feel...
- a) **More awkward than it turns out.** (correct)
- b) About as awkward as it turns out.
- c) Less awkward than it turns out.

### Teach card (27 words)

> People expect deep talk with someone new to feel awkward. In studies, it felt less awkward and more connecting
> than expected. Others cared more than people guessed.

### Show me

Ask: "Tap the first step past small talk."

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| 1 | Small talk: "Which branch are you in?" |
| 2 | One step deeper: "Why did you pick it?" **(spot)** |
| 3 | Deeper: "What do you hope it leads to?" |
| 4 | Share back: "Honestly, I picked mine for my parents. Still figuring out if I like it." |

Feedback: "Part 2 asks for a reason, not a fact. Part 4 matters too: sharing your own answer keeps it two-way."

### Guided try

Priya and her new PG roommate have done the "where are you from" round. What is a good next step?

| Option | Correct | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| a) "What's one thing you're looking forward to this year?" | Yes | It is personal but easy to answer, one step deeper. |
| b) Stick to the weather. New people find personal stuff boring. | No, **misconception: "Others won't care about deeper talk."** | In the studies, partners cared more than people expected. |
| c) "What does your family earn?" | No | That is private, not deep. Deep means meaningful, not intrusive. |

### Do it today

Ask one "why" or "what do you hope" question to someone you usually only small-talk with. Share your own answer
too.

Reflection prompt: "Was it as awkward as you expected?"

### Later-day question

"In the studies, why did people avoid deeper talk?" (Answer: they expected others to care less than they did.)

### Editor note

Family functions in India often bring intrusive questions (marks, salary, marriage). The lesson can name the
difference: those are private facts, not deep talk. Do not suggest asking anyone about caste, income, religion
or marriage plans.

## 4. Keep a new name

### Evidence

| Claim | Source quote | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Recalling names in a "name game" beat normal introductions, even months later. | "the name game participants were much better at remembering one another's names after 30 min, 2 weeks, and 11 months." | Morris & Fritz (2000), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 6(2). https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/18865 |
| The gain came from recalling, not from hearing names more often. | "A second experiment tested the contribution of retrieval practice by comparing 2 versions of the name game with a procedure that was matched for number of repetitions and time spent on the task. Again, the name games were superior." | Same |
| A short refresher later helped further. | "The reversed name game was superior to the original name game and the refresher rounds benefited all groups." | Morris & Fritz (2002), *Memory* 10(4). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12097210/ |

**Strength: moderate.** Strong for group introductions in classes (the setting tested). Using it for one name at a
time in daily life is our inference from the retrieval-practice finding. We did not find an open source for the
"next-in-line effect" (forgetting names said just before your own turn), so it is left out.

### Hook question

New hostel wing, 10 names in 10 minutes. What helps most to remember them next week?
- a) Hearing each name many times.
- b) **Trying to recall the names yourself, a little later.** (correct)
- c) Writing them down once.

### Teach card (27 words)

> To keep a new name, pull it from memory: say it back soon, then try again a little later. Recalling works
> better than just hearing it again.

### Show me

Ask: "Tap the step where he pulls the name from memory."

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| 1 | Orientation day. "Hi, I'm Farhan." |
| 2 | "Farhan, which floor are you on?" |
| 3 | At lunch, before checking the group list: "Farhan, Neha, Vikram..." **(spot)** |
| 4 | Next morning: "Hi Farhan!" |

Feedback: "Part 3 recalls the names with no help. That effort is what made names stick in the studies."

### Guided try

Aditi joins a club meet with 8 new people. Which plan fits the research?

| Option | Correct | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| a) Repeat each name in her head ten times right away. | No, **misconception: "Repeating is the same as recalling."** | When repeats were matched, recalling still won. |
| b) After the meet, try to recall all 8, then check the list. | Yes | Recall first, then check. That is retrieval practice. |
| c) Do nothing. Names stick if you care enough. | No | Caring helps attention, but the studies point to recall practice. |

### Do it today

Learn one new name today. Use it once in the chat. Later, recall it before you check.

Reflection prompt: "Did the name come back the next day?"

### Later-day question

Show a face-name card from the lesson scene and ask the learner to recall the name before revealing it. (The
question itself is retrieval practice.)

### Editor note

Add a line that asking "Remind me your name?" is fine. It removes the fear that forgetting is rude.

## 5. Disagree kindly

### Evidence

| Claim | Source quote | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Receptiveness is a way of talking, and it can be measured. | "the use of language to communicate one's willingness to thoughtfully engage with opposing views." | Yeomans, Minson, Collins, Chen & Gino (2020), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 160, 131-148. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.03.011 (read from the accepted manuscript: https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/handle/10044/1/85402) |
| The four-part recipe. | "positive statements, rather than negations; explicit acknowledgement of understanding; finding points of agreement; and hedging to soften claims." | Same, Study 4 |
| People using it were seen as better partners and were more persuasive. | "writers who follow the recipe are seen as more desirable partners for future collaboration and their messages are seen as more persuasive" | Same, abstract |
| Being receptive early prevents fights later. | "conversational receptiveness at the beginning of a conversation forestalls conflict escalation at the end." | Same, abstract |
| We are poor judges of our own receptiveness. | Executives were rated more receptive "according to our algorithm and their partners, but not their own self-evaluations" | Same, abstract |
| It feels harder to do. | "following the recipe is difficult, and they would be less likely to communicate in a receptive manner in the future." | Same, Study 4 discussion |

**Strength: strong** for written disagreements on policy topics, US samples. Speaking to elders in person, in an
Indian family, was not tested.

### Hook question

In an argument, does sounding open make you less convincing?
- a) Yes, it looks weak.
- b) **No. In one study, opponents rated open replies as more persuasive.** (correct)
- c) It makes no difference.

### Teach card (26 words)

> To disagree kindly: show you understood, name a point you agree on, soften with "I think", and say what you
> want, not just what is wrong.

### Show me

Group project. Rohan wants to split the slides by topic. Neha disagrees. Ask: "Tap the line that finds common
ground."

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| 1 | "I get it, splitting by topic means everyone owns a part." (acknowledge) |
| 2 | "And we both want it done before Friday." (agree) **(spot)** |
| 3 | "I think the slides might look uneven, though." (soften) |
| 4 | "What if we split by topic and one person does a final design pass?" (positive ask) |

Feedback: "Part 2 finds a shared goal. Neha still disagrees, but Rohan can hear it."

### Guided try

At a family function, an uncle says: "Engineering is the only safe career." Kunal studies design. Which reply is
receptive and still keeps Kunal's view?

| Option | Correct | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| a) "That's just not true, Uncle. That's outdated." | No, **misconception: "Blunt is more convincing."** | Plain negation scored lower on receptiveness and persuasion. |
| b) "I see why it feels safe, Uncle. We both want me to have a steady job. I think design can do that too. Can I show you where my seniors got placed?" | Yes | It acknowledges, agrees, softens, and offers something positive. |
| c) "Okay, Uncle." (and says nothing more) | No, **misconception: "Kind means giving in."** | Receptive is not the same as agreeing. Kunal can keep his view. |

### Do it today

Next time you disagree today, in chat or in person, start with one line that shows you understood the other view.

Reflection prompt: "Did the reply get calmer or hotter?"

### Later-day question

"Which of these is a hedge?" ("I think...", "Obviously...", "Never...")

## 6. End a chat well

### Evidence

| Claim | Source quote | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Chats rarely end when either person wants. | "conversations almost never ended when both conversants wanted them to and rarely ended when even one conversant wanted them to" | Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney & Wilson (2021), *PNAS* 118(10). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7958231/ |
| We cannot tell when the other person wants to stop. | "Conversants had little idea when their partners wanted to end and underestimated how discrepant their partners' desires were from their own." | Same |
| Among strangers (Study 2), in almost half of chats both wanted to stop sooner. | "In 46.83% of the conversations, both conversants wanted the conversation to end before it did, and in 9.52% of the conversations, both participants wanted the conversation to end after it did." | Same, Study 2 |
| Honest signals could help, but people rarely give them. | "coordination problems are readily solved when players can communicate honestly" ... "honestly communicating a desire to terminate a conversation may itself be a social rupture." | Same, introduction |
| After chats, people underrate how much they were liked, including first-year students and their hostel-mates. | "people systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them and enjoyed their company" ... "as first-year college students got to know their dorm mates" | Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark (2018), *Psychological Science* 29(11). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30183512/ |

**Strength: moderate.** The mismatch finding is solid (932 conversations, US). The paper did **not** test exit
lines. "A clear, warm exit helps" is our inference from the authors' coordination-problem point. Mark it as such
in the pack's fact-check.

### Hook question

In a study of chats between strangers, how often did both people want it to end sooner than it did?
- a) Almost never.
- b) About 1 in 10.
- c) **Almost half the time.** (correct)

### Teach card (30 words)

> Most chats don't end when either person wants, and we can't tell what the other wants. A clear, warm exit is
> fine. They likely liked you more than you think.

### Show me

Hostel common room. Ask: "Tap the clear exit."

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| 1 | "I've got to call home before 10." (a reason) |
| 2 | "Loved hearing about your trek." (a warm recap) |
| 3 | "Canteen tomorrow?" (a next step, only if you want one) |
| 4 | "See you, Meera!" **(spot)** |

Feedback: "Part 4 ends it clearly. The warm line before it helps it land well. Using her name ties back to idea 4."

### Guided try

At a college fest stall, Vikram has talked with a senior for 25 minutes. He wants to go. What fits the research?

| Option | Correct | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| a) Stay until the senior ends it. Leaving first is rude. | No, **misconception: "I can tell they want to keep going."** | People had little idea when partners wanted to stop. Often both wanted to stop sooner. |
| b) Say a warm line and a clear goodbye. | Yes | The senior may want to stop too. A clear exit helps both. |
| c) Keep checking his phone until the senior gets the hint. | No | Hints are hard to read. That is why chats run on. |

### Do it today

End one chat today on purpose: one warm line, one clear goodbye.

Reflection prompt: "Name one sign they enjoyed talking with you." (Uses the liking-gap finding without promising
anything.)

### Later-day question

"After a first chat, people tend to think they were liked... (less / about as much / more) than they were?"
(Answer: less.)

## 7. Ask a senior for advice

### Evidence

| Claim | Source quote | Source |
|---|---|---|
| People avoid asking for advice for fear of looking incompetent, and the fear is misplaced. | "We find that people are reticent to seek advice for fear of appearing incompetent. This fear, however, is misplaced." | Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer (2015), *Management Science* 61(6), 1421-1435. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.2054 (abstract read via Crossref: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mnsc.2014.2054; publisher page blocked) |
| Advice-seekers are seen as more competent. | "We demonstrate that individuals perceive those who seek advice as more competent than those who do not." | Same |
| It depends on the ask: hard task, asked personally, asked of an expert. | "Individuals perceive those who seek advice as more competent when the task is difficult rather than when it is easy, when people seek advice from them personally rather than when they seek advice from others and when people seek advice from experts rather than from nonexperts or not at all." | Same |

**Strength: moderate.** We read the abstract only; methods and effect sizes not checked. Lab tasks, not real
workplaces. Indian office hierarchy and job interviews were not tested. Using it with interviewers (asking them a
question about their own work) is an untested inference; leave it out of the claim and keep it as an optional
example only.

### Hook question

At your internship you are stuck on a hard task. Does asking your senior for advice make you look less capable?
- a) Yes, it shows you can't cope.
- b) **No. In studies, people who asked for advice on hard tasks were seen as more competent.** (correct)
- c) Only if you ask more than once.

### Teach card (29 words)

> Asking an expert for advice on a hard task did not make people look weak in studies. They were seen as more
> competent, most when the task was hard.

### Show me

Internship. Meera is stuck on a data-cleaning step. Ask: "Tap the part that asks for their expertise."

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| 1 | "I tried two fixes and both broke the dates." (shows effort) |
| 2 | "You've built these pipelines. What would you check first?" (asks them, for their expertise) **(spot)** |
| 3 | She asks her team lead directly, not "anyone know?" in the group. |
| 4 | Next day: "Your tip worked. Thanks." |

Feedback: "Part 2 asks this person, on a hard problem, about something they know well. Those are the conditions
where advice-seeking looked most competent."

### Guided try

First week of his internship, Rohan can't find an Excel setting he could look up in a minute. What fits the
research?

| Option | Correct | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| a) Always ask. Asking always looks smart. | No, **misconception: "The effect works for any question."** | It held most for hard tasks, not easy ones. |
| b) Look it up first. Save advice asks for hard problems the person knows well. | Yes | That matches when advice-seeking looked most competent. |
| c) Never ask. Solve everything alone to look capable. | No, **misconception: "Asking means I'm weak."** | The study found this fear was misplaced. |

### Do it today

Ask one person with more experience for advice on something truly hard for you. Say what you have already tried.

Reflection prompt: "What did you learn that you could not have looked up?"

### Later-day question

"When did asking for advice look most competent?" (Pick: hard task / easy task / asked the whole group.)

## 8. Reach out one-to-one (WhatsApp groups)

### Evidence

| Claim | Source quote | Source |
|---|---|---|
| People underrate how much others value a short check-in or small gesture. | "we document a robust underestimation of how much other people appreciate being reached out to." | Liu, Rim, Min & Min (2023), *JPSP* 124(4), 754-771. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35816566/ |
| The gap is bigger with people you are less close to, and when it is a surprise. | "it is magnified when the reach-out context is more surprising: when it occurs within a surprising (vs. unsurprising) context for the recipient and when it occurs between more socially distant (vs. socially close) others." | Same |
| Group size: in critical situations, others being present makes each person less likely to help. | "the presence of other people in a critical situation reduces the likelihood that an individual will help." | Fischer et al. (2011), *Psychological Bulletin* 137(4). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21534650/ |
| But this is weaker when the others are people you know. | The effect was reduced "when the bystanders were not strangers." | Same |

**Strength: moderate** for "reach out one-to-one" (preregistered experiments, but on brief messages and gifts,
not WhatsApp itself). **Weak** for any claim about group chats. The bystander meta-analysis is about
emergencies, and its own moderators suggest a weaker effect among friends. Two papers on chat groups and
e-mail could not be opened (see Sources), so they are not used. Do not teach "messages in groups get ignored" as a
fact.

### Hook question

You text an old school friend: "Saw this and thought of you." How much will they value it, compared with your
guess?
- a) Less than you guess.
- b) About what you guess.
- c) **More than you guess.** (correct)

### Teach card (29 words)

> People underrate how much a short check-in message is valued, most when it comes from someone they rarely
> talk to. A one-to-one note can mean more than you expect.

### Show me

Ask: "Tap the move from group to one-to-one."

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| 1 | The school group shares an old farewell photo. Aditi spots Kunal in it. |
| 2 | Instead of only reacting in the group, she opens a chat with Kunal. **(spot)** |
| 3 | "Saw the farewell photo. Remembered your mimicry of Sir. How's Pune?" |
| 4 | "No rush to reply." |

Feedback: "Part 2 moves it to one person. Part 3 is short and specific. Part 4 takes the pressure off both sides."

### Guided try

Neha wants to wish a hostel friend she hasn't spoken to in months. She thinks: "It'll be weird after so long."
What does the research suggest?

| Option | Correct | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| a) It will likely be valued more than she expects. | Yes | The surprise of hearing from her is part of why it lands well. |
| b) Skip it. Only close friends value messages. | No, **misconception: "Distance makes it unwelcome."** | The gap was larger with less-close people. |
| c) Post a wish in the big group instead. It's the same. | No | The study looked at one-to-one reach-outs. A group post was not tested. |

### Do it today

Send one short, no-reply-needed message to someone you haven't talked to in a while. Then close the app.

Reflection prompt: "Did they reply? How did it feel to send it?"

### Later-day question

"People who reach out tend to (under / over) estimate how much it is appreciated." (Answer: under.)

### Editor note

"Then close the app" matters. NANDI replaces night scrolling, so the mission must not become a reason to stay in
WhatsApp. Suggest a daytime nudge only.

## Cross-cutting risks for editors

| Risk | What to do |
|---|---|
| Samples are mostly US, UK, Israel. Indian hierarchy (elders, seniors, managers) is untested. | Say "in studies" in copy. Keep Indian scenes as practice settings, not as proof. |
| Track name "Talk to anyone" promises more than the evidence. | Consider an honest name, e.g. "Easier conversations". (Job 4 covers naming.) |
| Techniques used as tricks. Huang's effect works through real responsiveness. | Frame every idea as interest in the other person. No "make them like you" copy. |
| Safety, especially for women. Several studies used strangers. | Missions use people already in your life (classmates, cousins, colleagues). Never require approaching strangers. |
| Social anxiety. Some learners will find every mission hard. | Every mission skippable with no cost and no streak. Offer a smaller version ("ask one follow-up in a text"). No health or therapy claims. A neutral line pointing to the college counsellor is fine. |
| Guilt copy. | No telling the learner what they ought to do, no "most people fail at this". Reflections ask what happened, not whether the learner did well. |
| Missions that pull people back into their phones. | Missions 8 and 1 can happen in chat; add "then close the app". No missions after the user's bedtime. |

## Myths not to teach

| Myth | What the evidence says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "A phone on the table ruins the chat." | Close replication failed. | Linares & Sellier 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34106931/ |
| "Paraphrasing makes people feel understood." | Mixed: no effect alone in 2010; the full package helped in 2014, small effects. | Weger 2010; Weger 2014 |
| "The more questions, the better." | The authors suggest too many questions without sharing back may lower liking (not tested). | Huang 2017 |
| "Asking for help always looks smart." | Depends on task difficulty and who you ask. | Brooks 2015 |
| "Being kind in a debate means giving in." | Receptive writers kept their view and were more persuasive. | Yeomans 2020 |
