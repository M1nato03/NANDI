# cj4 · Packaging: why those videos feel worth it, done honestly

Job 4 of batch 4 (idea engine, 23 Oct 2026). Branch `ideas/cj4-packaging`.
Scope: name the pull in Jenil's example titles, check it against research, then rewrite
NANDI's 6 topic names and 10 lesson hooks so they stay curious but honest.
No app code or content JSON changed. Everything below is a proposal for editors.

---

## 1. Short answer

- The example titles work through six pulls: an outcome promise, an identity, a curiosity gap,
  a specific number, proof from a person, and a story or known character.
- Research supports two of these well for learning: **a real knowledge gap** (Loewenstein 1994,
  Kang et al. 2009, Gruber et al. 2014) and **guess, then feedback** (Loewenstein 1994;
  St Hilaire et al. 2024). NANDI already has the second in its step hooks.
- The risk is the gap between the promise and the payoff. Curiosity has a "tendency to
  disappoint when satisfied" (Loewenstein). Clickbait lowered trust in several experiments, but
  not all of them, and the effects were small.
- YouTube now removes videos whose title "promises viewers something that the video
  doesn't deliver", starting in India.
- Rule for NANDI: **promise only what the same 2-minute lesson delivers, and make the gap
  come from a real, sourced surprise, not from hiding a noun.**
- 43 of NANDI's 54 concept hooks are statements, not questions. Many state the answer
  before the lesson starts (habits, sleep and memory, sky colours, pressure cooker).
  So the topic card opens no gap. The rewrites below fix 10 of them.

## 3. What the research says

### 3.1 Curiosity is a felt gap (Loewenstein 1994 and later)

Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation.
*Psychological Bulletin* 116(1), 75-98.
Opened: https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf
(a scanned PDF with no text layer; read as page images, pp. 75, 87-92).

| Quote | Page | What it means for NANDI |
|---|---|---|
| "The new account interprets curiosity as a form of cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding." | 75 (abstract) | A hook has to make a gap felt, not just state a fact |
| "Such information gaps produce the feeling of deprivation labeled *curiosity*. The curious individual is motivated to obtain the missing information to reduce or eliminate the feeling of deprivation." | 87 | Curiosity is mildly unpleasant. Close it fast |
| "The posing of a question or presentation of a riddle or puzzle confronts the individual directly with missing information and is therefore perhaps the most straightforward curiosity inducer." | 91 | A question hook is the simplest honest trigger |
| "Exposure to a sequence of events with an anticipated but unknown resolution will almost inevitably create curiosity to know the outcome." … "Such curiosity is exacerbated when an individual generates a prediction" | 91 | Why the detective-case title works; a guess raises the pull |
| "The violation of expectations often triggers a search for an explanation" | 91 | Lead with the surprising fact as a question |
| "Possession of information by someone else also causes curiosity." | 91 | The pull of "how I won 11 hackathons" |
| "curiosity is unlikely to arise in the absence of an existing knowledge base and … the likelihood of experiencing curiosity should increase as an individual obtains information about a particular topic." | 89 | Start from what an 18-25 Indian already knows (idli prices, UPI, hostel fans) |
| "people will not expose themselves to curiosity-inducing situations in which there is only a slim chance of satisfying the curiosity or in which there would be a long delay before the information is received." | 90 | People choose a hook only if they trust it will pay off. A cliffhanger to tomorrow breaks that trust |
| "the combination of guessing and feedback increased curiosity substantially." | 92 | NANDI's guess-first step hook is the right shape |
| "the disappointment often experienced when curiosity is satisfied can also be explained by the fact that curiosity is driven by deprivation." | 92 | Over-hyped hooks set up a let-down. Keep the promise smaller than the payoff |

Later work:

- **Curiosity peaks at medium confidence.** Kang, M. J., et al. (2009). The wick in the candle
  of learning. *Psychological Science* 20(8), 963-973.
  Opened: https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf
  - "Reported curiosity was indeed an inverted-U-shaped function of P, reaching its maximum
    when P was around .50"
  - "subjects spent more scarce resources (either limited tokens or waiting time) to find out
    answers when they were more curious."
  - "Higher curiosity in an initial session was correlated with better recall of surprising
    answers 1 to 2 weeks later."
  - For NANDI: a hook works best when a learner could guess either way. Unknowable trivia
    (no idea) and obvious facts (sure) both pull less.
- **Curiosity helps memory.** Gruber, M. J., Gelman, B. D., & Ranganath, C. (2014). States of
  curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit. *Neuron*.
  doi 10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060. Abstract opened via Europe PMC
  (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:25284006%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json;
  PubMed itself returned a cookie wall).
  - "participants showed improved memory for information that they were curious about and
    for incidental material learned during states of high curiosity."
- **Guessing first helps the guessed fact.** St Hilaire, K. J., Chan, J. C. K., & Ahn, D. (2024).
  Guessing as a learning intervention. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*.
  doi 10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8. Abstract opened via Europe PMC.
  - "This preregistered meta-analysis demonstrated a moderate specific effect (g = 0.54, k = 97)
    but a virtually nonexistent general effect (g = 0.04, k = 91)."
  - For NANDI: the hook question must be about the exact idea the lesson teaches.
- **Clickbait is named as an information-gap case.** Golman, R., & Loewenstein, G. (2016/2018).
  Information gaps: A theory of preferences regarding the presence and absence of
  information. *Decision*. Opened:
  https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/Information%20Gaps--Golman%20and%20Loewenstein.pdf
  - The paper asks what the mind is consuming "when we observe people succumbing to clickbait
    on the Internet".
  - "only after reading the headline does the person become aware of this uncertainty (and
    hence become curious to resolve it)."
  - For NANDI: the hook creates the gap. That puts the duty to fill it on us.
- **Curiosity can be used against people.** Hsee, C. K., & Ruan, B. (2016). The Pandora effect.
  *Psychological Science*. doi 10.1177/0956797616631733. Abstract opened via Europe PMC.
  - "curiosity could lead humans to expose themselves to aversive stimuli (even electric
    shocks) for no apparent benefits."
  - For NANDI: an open gap is strong enough to override self-interest. An app that is meant
    to beat late-night scrolling must not leave gaps open at night.
- **Stories lower scrutiny.** Green, M. C., & Brock, T. C. (2000). The role of transportation
  in the persuasiveness of public narratives. *JPSP*. doi 10.1037//0022-3514.79.5.701.
  Abstract opened via Europe PMC.
  - "highly transported readers found fewer false notes in a story than less-transported
    readers."
  - For NANDI: detective and story hooks need the same fact check as plain ones, maybe more.

### 3.2 Forward reference: the "secret" trick

- Blom, J. N., & Hansen, K. R. (2015). Click bait: Forward-reference as lure in online news
  headlines. *Journal of Pragmatics* 76, 87-100. Abstract opened:
  https://www.ucviden.dk/da/publications/click-bait-forward-reference-as-lure-in-online-news-headlines/
  - Journalists "use the forward-referring technique as a stylistic and narrative luring
    device", "trying to induce anticipation and curiosity so the readers click".
  - "commercialization and tabloidization seem to lead to a recurrent use of forward-reference".
- "A Sudoku secret" and "this one trick" are forward reference: the gap comes from a hidden
  word, not from a real surprise. NANDI should not use it.

### 3.3 What gets clicks (headline field experiments)

- Banerjee, A., & Urminsky, O. *The Language That Drives Engagement*. Published in
  *Marketing Science* 44(3), 2025. I could open only the January 2021 working paper:
  https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf. Its cover says
  "THIS VERSION REPORTS PRELIMINARY RESULTS FROM AN EXPLORATORY DATASET" and
  "FINAL RESULTS FROM CONFIRMATORY DATASET ARE LIKELY TO DIFFER". Treat these as leads.
  - "using forward reference language in headlines, which is predicted to increase curiosity,
    increased engagement with the headlines."
  - "headlines that incorporated information-seeking cues (e.g., by suggesting that the
    content would answer a question or teach something) performed more poorly."
  - "the presence of numbers in headlines increased click-through rates"
  - "making headlines more concrete improved engagement"
  - "headlines implying social stories about individuals were more effective, but writing
    headlines directed to the reader backfired."
- Gligorić, K., Lifchits, G., West, R., & Anderson, A. (2023). Linguistic effects on news
  headline success. *PLoS One* 18(3): e0281682. Opened:
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10038272/
  - "we find that length is positively associated with headline success"
  - "the presence of negative-emotion words is positively associated with headline success"
  - "we found no significant positive association between the use of second-person pronouns
    and headline success"
- What this means: clicks reward gaps, numbers, negativity and stories about people.
  Clicks are not NANDI's goal. NANDI's goal is less scrolling and earlier sleep. So copy the
  honest parts (concrete, specific, a person in a scene) and skip the rest (negativity,
  hidden nouns). "Learn X" wording may get fewer taps. That is an accepted cost.

### 3.4 Clickbait backlash: real, but small and mixed

| Study | Sample | Finding (quoted) | Opened |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vemuri & Kaushal, IIIT Hyderabad, "Clickbait – Trust and Credibility of Digital News", IEEE (c. 2021) | 200 (100 English-speaking Indian, 100 American) | "Clickbait headlines significantly reduced the credibility of news items." | Summary page only: https://blogs.iiit.ac.in/monthly_news/dr-kavita-vemuri-and-vivek-kaushals-paper-in-early-access-area-on-ieee-xplore/ |
| Song, Santos Silva, Suau & Espinosa-Anke (2025), arXiv 2503.11116, not peer reviewed | 1,601 (Spain, UK) | "The negative effect of clickbait titles on trust ratings (significant at p<.001)" | Full PDF: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11116 |
| Center for Media Engagement, UT Austin (Scacco & Muddiman) | 2,057 US adults | "Question-based headlines lead to more negative attitudes about the headline". "Forward-reference headlines did not lead to different reactions, expectations, or anticipated engagement" | https://mediaengagement.org/research/clickbait-headlines/ |
| Same team, outrage headlines (2018) | 1,535 | Trust 3.43 vs 3.51 out of 5 (outrage vs not): a small dip. "Outrage headlines prompted readers to think that the news source was less engaged with the interests of their communities." | https://newscollab.org/2019/06/04/outrageous-clickbait-content-can-deter-long-term-engagement-study-finds/ |
| Munger, Luca, Nagler & Tucker (2020), *Public Opinion Quarterly* 84(1) | Facebook-ad and MTurk samples | "reading clickbait headlines does not drive affective polarization, information retention, or trust in media." | Abstract: https://csmapnyu.org/research/the-null-effects-of-clickbait-headlines-on-polarization-trust-and-learning |
| Molyneux & Coddington (2019), *Journalism Practice* | Two experiments | Search snippet only: clickbait "may lower" credibility and quality; effect described as small | **Could not open** (Temple repository returned 403) |

Two notes:

- The question-headline finding is from news, where "Individuals in some ways expect the news
  to answer questions for them, not necessarily pose questions" (Scacco, quoted at
  https://www.journalism.co.uk/readers-perceive-question-based-headlines-more-negatively-study-shows/).
  A learning app is different: the learner opens it to be asked, and the answer comes in
  seconds. The prequestion evidence (3.1) is the better guide for NANDI.
- The clearest cost is the broken promise, not the gap itself. YouTube's India-first policy
  (Dec 2024) aims at titles or thumbnails that promise "something that the video doesn't
  deliver", because "This can leave viewers feeling tricked, frustrated, or even misled"
  (quoted in https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/18/youtube-says-it-will-start-cracking-down-on-videos-with-clickbait-titles-in-india).

### 3.5 India's dark-pattern rules

The CCPA's *Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023* were notified on
30 November 2023. I could not open the official PDF (consumeraffairs.nic.in returned 503 and a
connection reset). These wordings come from Trilegal's summary (26 Dec 2023, opened as a PDF
from https://trilegal.com/knowledge_repository/guidelines-for-prevention-and-regulation-of-dark-patterns-2023/).
The guidelines target commercial gain, but NANDI's own rule is stricter, so the wording is
still a useful line to stay behind:

- False urgency: "Creating or implying a false sense of urgency or scarcity to mislead a user
  into immediately taking an action".
- Confirm shaming: "Using emotionally charged design tactics to create a sense of fear, shame,
  ridicule or guilt in a user's mind".
- Bait and switch: "Baiting the user with a particular outcome which is deceptively switched
  to show an alternate outcome."
- Nagging: "Disrupting or annoying users without their permission through repetitive and
  persistent requests, information, options or notifications".

A hook that promises one outcome and then teaches another is the content version of bait and
switch.

## 4. Rules for honest hooks (proposed editorial checklist)

| # | Rule | Why (source) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The hook's promise is delivered inside the same lesson. No "find out tomorrow". | Slim chance or long delay puts people off (Loewenstein p. 90); broken promise (YouTube policy) |
| 2 | The gap comes from a real, sourced surprise, never from a hidden noun ("this trick", "a secret"). | Forward reference is a lure (Blom & Hansen) |
| 3 | Pitch the question so a learner could guess either way. | Curiosity peaks near 50% confidence (Kang et al.) |
| 4 | The hook asks about the exact idea the lesson teaches. | Prequestion benefit is specific: g = 0.54 vs 0.04 (St Hilaire et al.) |
| 5 | Scoped outcome verbs: spot, check, compare, work out, see why. Never "every", "anyone", "always", "fix", "hack", "secret". | Over-promise sets up disappointment (Loewenstein p. 92) |
| 6 | Numbers only if already in the concept's claims and `hookSourceRefs`. | Numbers pull clicks (Banerjee & Urminsky draft), so they must be true |
| 7 | Name the setting (hostel, exam, UPI, canteen), not a flaw in the reader ("why you're always tired"). | Talking to the reader did not help clicks (Gligorić; Banerjee draft); avoids shame copy |
| 8 | No health, body or money outcome promises. Teach the check, not the result. | NANDI hard rule; CCPA bait and switch |
| 9 | Stories use original Indian characters and places; no franchise names. | Original-theme rule; stories lower scrutiny (Green & Brock) |
| 10 | Keep validator limits: hook ≤ 280 characters, Flesch ≥ 60, at least one `hookSourceRefs` entry. | `src/data/packs/schemas.ts`, `validate.ts` |

## 6. Ten hooks: current text and rewrites

### 6.1 Psychology · `topic.psychology.json` · `habits`

- Current: "Missing one day did not undo a new habit in Lally's 12-week study. Habits were estimated
  to take from 18 to 254 days to settle in."
- Problem: gives away the answer, so there is no gap.
- Rewrite: **You missed one day of a new habit. Is it back to zero? See what a 12-week study found.**
- Hinglish: *Naye habit ka ek din miss ho gaya. Kya sab zero ho gaya? Dekho 12 hafte ki ek study mein kya mila.*
- Pull: violated expectation, guess. Refs: `lally`.

### 6.2 Psychology · `topic.psychology.json` · `variable-rewards`

- Current: "A surprise good post can make the next swipe tempting, even after several dull ones."
- Problem: a fine scene, but no question and no outcome.
- Rewrite: **Why is the next swipe so hard to skip? See how surprise rewards keep you checking,
  and spot them in your apps.**
- Hinglish: *Agla swipe chhodna itna mushkil kyun lagta hai? Dekho surprise rewards kaise baar-baar check karwate hain, aur apne apps mein unhe pehchano.*

### 6.3 Psychology · `topic.psychology.json` · `spotlight-effect`

### 6.4 Money · `topic.money-markets.json` · `compounding`

- Current: "₹1 lakh growing at an example rate of 7% for 30 years becomes about ₹7.6 lakh. The last
  ten years add more than the first twenty."
- Problem: a strong surprise, but it is stated, not asked.
- Rewrite: **₹1 lakh grows at an example 7% for 30 years. Do the last 10 years add more than the

## 7. Which hooks break the no-dark-patterns rule if pushed further

"Pushed" = the next step a growth-minded editor might take. Each example version below is
**not allowed**.

| Hook | Pushed version (not allowed) | Rule it breaks | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| compounding | "Turn ₹1 lakh into ₹7.6 lakh. Every year you wait costs you lakhs." | Money promise (returns are not fixed: `sip` claim); false urgency; loss framing | High |
| fad-diet-claims | "Lose 5 kg the safe way" / "Why your diet keeps you fat" | Health promise; body shame | High |
| sleep-recovery | "Why you're always tired, and the fix for 8 hours ≠ rested" | Health promise; diagnoses the reader | High |
| habits | "Missed a day? Fine. Miss a week and you lose it all." | Loss state; streak pressure; fear | High |
| variable-rewards | "Your phone is hacking your dopamine" | Pseudo-science; fear. NANDI also bans random rewards itself, so it cannot blame apps while using them | High |
| spotlight-effect | "Never feel embarrassed again" / "Nobody notices you" | False promise about feelings; can hurt | Medium |
| upi-scams | "Scammers can empty your account in 10 seconds" / "Only 1% know this" | Fear; made-up number; fake social proof | Medium |
| company-trade-rule | "The shocking way Britain looted India" | Outrage bait; unsourced framing | Medium |
| indian-place-value | "The Indian secret the West stole" | Hidden-noun bait; overclaim | Low-medium |
| ceiling-fans | "This fan trick halves your power bill" | Unsourced money promise; "trick" bait | Low-medium |

Pattern-level bans, worth adding to the editorial review:

- Cliffhangers across days ("answer tomorrow"). This is an open gap at bedtime (Hsee & Ruan),
  and it works like a nag.
- Hidden-noun hooks ("this one trick", "a secret").
- Absolute promises ("every", "anyone", "always").
- Reader-deficit framing ("why you're always…", "stop being…").
- Franchise characters or titles in hooks.
