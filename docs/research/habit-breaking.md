## Evidence on changing habits

- **Formation takes variable time.** In Lally and colleagues’ study, people repeated a chosen behavior in the same context; modeled time to reach near-maximum automaticity ranged from **18 to 254 days**. Missing one opportunity did not materially disrupt formation. The range is an estimate from those behaviors, not a deadline for quitting Reels. [Lally et al., 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674)

- **Cues matter more as a habit develops.** “Cue → routine → reward” is a useful shorthand: rewards help establish repetition, while familiar contexts can later trigger the response without a conscious goal. For night scrolling, the cue might be getting into bed with the phone; identifying the actual cue is the first design task. [Wood & Neal, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17907866/)

- **Make a response plan at the cue.** Implementation intentions specify *“If situation X occurs, then I will do Y.”* A meta-analysis of 94 tests found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (**d = 0.65**), across varied goals rather than specifically phone use. An example is: “If I reach for Instagram after lights-out, I will start my saved audio instead.” [Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601%2806%2938002-1)

- **Change access and offer a substitute.** Removing cues or adding friction can interrupt an unwanted response; repeatedly doing an appealing alternative at the same cue may build a competing association. Evidence for habit substitution is promising but less direct than the evidence for cue-based habit formation. Suppressing thoughts alone can backfire: a chocolate-thought suppression experiment found subsequent behavioral rebound. [Gardner, 2024](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12975); [Erskine, 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0195666307003674)

- **Make the alternative pleasant now.** In Milkman and colleagues’ gym experiment, restricting tempting audiobooks to workouts initially increased visits by **51%** versus control; the advantage faded over time. This supports trying a favorite audio story with a bedtime routine, while treating the phone application as an inference. [Milkman et al., 2014](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784)

- **Feeds provide uncertain rewards and few stopping points.** A large study linked social feedback, such as likes, to reward learning in *posting*; it does not establish that every Reel works like a slot machine. A 2026 short-video survey links anticipated rewarding content and recommendations with compulsive scrolling. Infinite feeds also remove natural stopping cues, making a deliberate endpoint a plausible intervention. [Lindström et al., 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-19607-x); [Wang et al., 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378720626000169); [Alutaybi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18803)

- **Digital tools can reduce measured use, with limits to the evidence.** In a randomized field study (**N = 112**), grayscale immediately reduced objectively measured screen time; self-set time limits produced a smaller, slower reduction, and neither change showed an immediate wellbeing or academic benefit. A three-week randomized trial (**N = 70**) of prompts after user-set app limits found about **29 fewer minutes per day** on participants’ most problematic app, but no significant change in problematic social-media-use scores. [Zimmermann & Sobolev, 2023](https://cris.technion.ac.il/en/publications/digital-strategies-for-screen-time-reduction-a-randomized-field-e/); [Mertens et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41950504/)

- **A pause can prevent an opening.** Among **280** one sec users, **36%** of attempted target-app openings were dismissed at the prompt; actual openings were **57% lower** after six weeks. The field comparison lacked a randomized no-app group, and an app creator coauthored the study. A separate randomized online experiment found the option to dismiss worked better than a deliberation message alone. [Grüning et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36795756/)

- **Treat a lapse as one event.** The “what-the-hell” or abstinence-violation account describes how interpreting one slip as total failure can promote further slipping, although relapse findings are mixed. In eating research, a brief self-compassion prompt reduced distress and subsequent eating among highly restrictive eaters; applying that result to scrolling remains a design hypothesis. [Kirchner et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289/); [Adams & Leary, 2007](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/782823)

- **Streaks motivate and can punish.** Across seven studies, displaying an intact streak increased subsequent engagement relative to displaying a broken one, even when past behavior was held constant; allowing repair softened the effect. A “perfect nights” counter may therefore make one late scroll feel disproportionately costly. [Silverman & Barasch, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029)

## Eight design rules for a night-scrolling replacement app

1. **Ask users to name their bedtime cue and choose one small replacement action.** [Wood & Neal, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17907866/); [Lally et al., 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674)
2. **Turn that choice into a visible “If I reach for Reels, then…” plan.** [Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601%2806%2938002-1)
3. **Put the replacement one tap away: saved audio, a short reading, or a calming activity the user chooses.** [Gardner, 2024](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12975); [Milkman et al., 2014](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784)
4. **At a targeted app opening, insert a brief pause with a clear “go back” option.** [Grüning et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36795756/)
5. **Offer optional bedtime grayscale and user-set app limits; let users adjust them.** [Zimmermann & Sobolev, 2023](https://cris.technion.ac.il/en/publications/digital-strategies-for-screen-time-reduction-a-randomized-field-e/); [Mertens et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41950504/)
6. **Give replacement content a definite end, followed by a “put phone down” prompt.** This applies the stopping-cue evidence to bedtime. [Alutaybi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18803)
7. **After a lapse, show “resume tonight” and the chosen next action; never label the night a failure.** [Lally et al., 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674); [Adams & Leary, 2007](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/782823)
8. **Track nights returned to the plan and minutes on the targeted apps, with repairable progress instead of a zero-reset streak.** [Silverman & Barasch, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029); [Mertens et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41950504/)
